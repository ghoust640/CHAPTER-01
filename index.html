01

THE CITY

NOVA KAELEN · 23:41 LOCAL · CEILING 400M

From four hundred metres the city looks like something that works. That’s the first lie Nova Kaelen tells, and she tells it beautifully. The residential stacks go up until the fog takes them, thirty, forty, sixty floors of lit windows, and above the fog the corporate spires keep going, clean and cold and lettered in company colours, so that the whole skyline reads like a stock index someone has stood on end.

The highways are laid between the towers rather than under them, ribbons of moving light strung from building to building, and at this hour they’re full. Nothing in Nova Kaelen is ever empty. The freight trains come out of the mountain tunnels in the north and run down along the Kaelen River, where the industrial lights burn a hard sodium orange that the rain smears into everything. The advertisements are the size of city blocks. They’re projected onto the flanks of the residential stacks, which means that four hundred thousand people sleep inside a slow rotation of teeth, watches, insurance, and patriotism.

Between the towers, small and patient, the drones. They fly the same eleven routes they have flown for nine years. You can set a clock by them, and the people down there do.

Look at it long enough from above and you will start to admire it. Everyone does. The reports written by men who have only ever seen Nova Kaelen from a transport window are full of the word remarkable.

The city looks prosperous from above.

It looks miserable from below.

02

BELOW THE LINE

SPRAWL DISTRICT · TERRACE 4 · GROUND

Terrace 4 never sees the sky. It sees the underside of the terrace above it, and the rain that comes down through the gaps in it, and it has learned to call that weather.

The streets here are narrow because they were never streets. They were service corridors for the towers, and then people moved into them, and then the city stopped pretending they would leave. Water runs where it wants. The stairwells have been load-bearing for two generations without being inspected once. Children play in the ribs of a decommissioned cargo lifter, and one of them has claimed the operator's cage as a house and charges the others to come inside.

Outside the clinic the line begins at four in the morning. By midnight it’s still forty people long, because the clinic runs on a quota and the quota was set in a building none of them will ever enter. Somebody has taped a hand-written notice to the door: NO RUST-DUST OVERDOSE AFTER 22:00. TRY TERRACE 6. Two blocks down, in a stairwell that smells of hot metal, that same trade is being conducted with the calm of a man selling bread.

At 23:52 a municipal drone comes down the corridor at head height and paints faces.

It works quickly. Most people don’t stop walking; they have learned that stopping is what gets noticed. A woman with a child on her hip is halfway past it when the tone changes, a small tone, almost polite.

MUNICIPAL UNIT M-114 · PUBLIC ADDRESS 23:52:16
“ADMINISTRATIVE DISCREPANCY DETECTED.”
“REMAIN WHERE YOU ARE. A CIVIL OFFICER IS EN ROUTE. ESTIMATED ARRIVAL: TWO MINUTES.”
[SUBJECT SPEAKS — 14s — NOT TRANSCRIBED, PRIORITY THRESHOLD NOT MET]
“YOUR COOPERATION HAS BEEN NOTED IN YOUR RECORD.”

The officer arrives in ninety seconds, which is faster than anything else in Terrace 4 has ever arrived. He’s not cruel. He doesn’t raise his voice. He asks her to come with him and he carries her bag for her, and someone takes the child, and the line outside the clinic watches without moving because moving is what gets noticed.

No execution. No screaming. A discrepancy, and a form, and a woman who doesn’t come back.

That’s what the machine is for. The guns are for later, and for people like G7.

03

THE SOUND

00:07 LOCAL · MIDWAY SUBSTATION 9

It arrives as pressure before it arrives as noise. Everyone on Terrace 4 feels it in the water pipes first, a single hard knock through the whole district, as though the city had swallowed wrong.

Then the lights go.

Not all of them. The advertisements stay up, because the advertisements are on a separate grid, and so for eleven seconds the only illumination in Terrace 4 is a woman's forty-metre face selling a savings account. People will describe that detail for years. It’s the part that gets into the songs.

When the emergency lighting catches, there’s gunfire somewhere north, in short disciplined bursts that stop and don’t start again. The crowd outside the clinic breaks and runs in four directions at once, and the drone that took the woman away is now overhead saying return to your residence to people who are already running towards their residences.

By 00:19 the public net has an answer. It comes with a graphic, and the graphic has already been made:

STATE BROADCAST · CHANNEL 1 · ALL DISTRICTS
SCI TERRORIST ACTIVITY — MIDWAY SUBSTATION 9
CASUALTIES UNDER ASSESSMENT. THE DIRECTORATE HAS AUTHORISED IMMEDIATE RESPONSE. CITIZENS ARE REMINDED THAT SHELTERING HOSTILE PERSONNEL IS AN OFFENCE UNDER ART. 88.
[INSIGNIA DISPLAYED — 6s — SCI, THREE BARS BROKEN]

Nobody on Terrace 4 knows what SCI wants. Ask ten people and you will get four answers and six shrugs. They know the insignia the way you know a weather symbol. They know that after an SCI night the checkpoints double and the clinic quota halves.

They know the city will be angry tomorrow, and that the anger will be pointed downward, at them, because that’s the only direction the city has ever learned to point it.

04

ALTITUDE

VTOL “ANVIL 2” · 01:14 · INBOUND

Inside the aircraft nobody is talking about the substation.

Vanguard has his weapon apart on his knee for the third time since take-off, not because anything is wrong with it but because his hands need somewhere to be. Patch goes down the bench in order, checking other people's kit and ignoring their opinions about it; she takes a tourniquet off Sledge, looks at it, and gives him a different one without explaining, and he lets her, which tells you everything about how that argument went the first time.

Sledge is unhappy about the timeline. Sledge is professionally unhappy about all timelines. “Nineteen minutes,” he says, to nobody. “They gave us nineteen minutes on a district we haven't walked. That's not a plan, that's a dare.”

Glitch has been inside the mission network since before the ramp closed. She has the district's camera trunk on her tablet and she’s not looking at the cameras; she’s looking at which cameras stopped reporting, and when, and in what order, because that’s a shape and shapes are her job.

Whisper reads. He reads the way some people pray. He has the intelligence packet open and he has already been through it twice, and he has that flat expression he gets when a document is well made and he doesn’t like it.

Trace has the window. He hasn’t moved in six minutes. Below them the terraces go past in layers, lit, unlit, lit, and he watches them the way you watch a river you fell into once.

And Rook sits with his back against the bulkhead and his eyes closed, and he’s not asleep, and everybody knows he’s not asleep, and nobody bothers him. He’s the reason the unit has a habit of coming home. He doesn’t talk on the way in. That’s simply understood, the way the tourniquet thing is understood.

06

THE WAY DOWN

01:38 → 02:11 · ON FOOT

They come in low over the river and go the rest of the way on foot, because the last two hundred metres of Terrace 4 can’t be flown; there’s roof over it.

Seven people in dark kit moving through a night market that hasn’t closed because it never closes. This is the part the training doesn’t cover. A man frying dough looks up at Sledge, and looks back down at the dough. A girl of maybe nine walks alongside Patch for half a block, matching her stride, until her mother takes her wrist without a word. Somebody's radio is playing the state broadcast on a loop; somebody else's is playing music, loudly, in defiance of it.

Nobody screams. Nobody calls out. That’s the thing Trace will mention in his debrief and nobody will write down: the district doesn’t react to armed men. It has been visited before.

They go through a residential block because the street outside it’s covered by a checkpoint that’s not theirs. Down a stairwell where the emergency lighting is a single strip of blue. Along a transit tunnel with the power cut, stepping over the third rail, past a whole family asleep in the alcove where the maintenance ladder used to be.

At 02:04 the market noise falls away behind them, and then something stranger happens.

The street ahead is empty.

Not quiet. Empty. Four blocks of Terrace 4 with nothing in them: no stalls, no dealers, no sleepers in the alcoves, no children in the lifter frame. There’s a pot still on a burner in a doorway. There’s a bicycle on its side with the wheel turning.

Vanguard puts a fist up. Everyone stops.

07

CONTACT

02:19 · WAREHOUSE 6 / SUB-LEVEL 2 → ROOF

The building is a pre-terrace freight house, six floors, poured concrete, built when the river still mattered. Somebody has spent money on it. The ground-floor windows have been bricked to a firing height. The loading bay is sandbagged from the inside. There’s a machine gun on the second-floor gallery with a clean field down the length of the approach, and it has been there long enough that the crew has brought up a chair.

Whisper counts eleven before they even breach. Then fourteen. Then he stops counting out loud, because it’s not useful any more and it’s bad for morale.

They go in through the sub-level, through a flood channel Glitch found on a 1974 municipal drawing that the district itself has forgotten, and the first four minutes are the only easy four minutes of the night.

Then the stairwell.

Concrete isn’t cover, it’s a delay; every round that comes down that stairwell arrives with a fistful of the wall attached to it. Sledge puts a charge on the landing door and the blast goes up the shaft instead of through it and takes the lights with it, and after that the fight happens in strobe: muzzle flash, the orange wash of an advertisement through a broken window, muzzle flash.

They fight up through the building the way you climb a ladder in a house fire. Patch works twice on the third floor, on the floor, with Trace holding a torch in his teeth. Vanguard takes the gallery because the gallery has to be taken and there’s no clever way to do it. Glitch, in a stairwell alcove with her back to the wall, breaks the building's own intercom and starts giving orders on it in Vesperian, and for ninety seconds a dozen SCI fighters obey a woman who’s not on their side.

Rain comes in through the shot-out windows on the fifth floor. There’s machinery up there, an old freight winch, still greased, and it turns slowly in the wind all through the last of the shooting, and every man in G7 will remember the sound of it and not the shots.

08

THE MANIFEST

02:47 · FIFTH FLOOR · CARGO STAGING

The shipment is staged wrong.

Rook notices it before anybody says a word about paperwork, because it’s a physical fact and he has spent nineteen years in rooms like this one. Cargo coming into a city is broken down: pallets opened, contents split, weight distributed small enough to move through a district on shoulders and handcarts. Cargo going out is consolidated: crated, banded, palletised, weighed, labelled, stacked by the door on the river side with the winch above it.

This is consolidated. All of it. Every crate on that floor is packed to leave.

They’re not smuggling weapons into Nova Kaelen. They’re moving them out of it.

And it’s not only weapons. Whisper gets the lids off four crates in ninety seconds and reads them out flat, one after another, like a man calling a stocktake:

Banded to the lid of C-01 is a transit document. It’s not a forgery. Forgeries are Whisper's speciality and he says so immediately and with a kind of offence: the paper is right, the register stamp is right, the routing block is right down to the internal courier code that changes every ninety days.

It’s a Vesperian military movement order. Someone with a signature block and an office and a pension authorised this.

Rook takes it because he’s nearest, and he stops.

The classification line across the top of the document is a format he has seen exactly once before, on a photocopy, in a solicitor's envelope, when he was fourteen years old.

09

LEDGER

02:53 · OFFICE, FIFTH FLOOR

They find the coordinator in a glass-walled foreman's office with his hands already on the desk. Fifties. Cardigan under a work coat. Reading glasses folded in his pocket. He looks like a man who audits things, which is exactly what he’s, and his rifle is on the floor three metres away where he put it before they came up the stairs.

He doesn’t beg. That’s the part that stays with all of them. Patch flexcuffs him and he thanks her.

Then he looks up at Rook, at the seven and the zero on his shoulder plate, and something goes across his face that’s not fear. It’s closer to pity, and it’s the single most unwelcome thing that happens all night.

10

HANDOVER

03:36 · RIVER APRON · EXFIL

The receiving party is already on the apron when they come out. That’s the second generous thing about the night.

They’re not Directorate. They’re not military police. They’re eight men in civilian rain shells with no unit patch, no name tape, and correct paperwork, and they have brought a sealed truck and a crate manifest that’s already filled in.

They take the coordinator. They take the crates. They take the transit document out of Rook's hand, and the man who takes it signs for it with a stamp rather than a name.

It goes smoothly, and it takes four minutes, and G7 stands on the apron in the rain afterwards with nothing in their hands.

Glitch is the one still working. She has the district's own record open, because Glitch never stops pulling on a thread once it has moved, and she says the first thing anyone has said in a while.

The state broadcast is still running the substation on a loop. By breakfast it’ll be a triumph, and the number of hostiles will be eight.

11 · FINAL

ROOK

05:12 · SECTION 7 · TERMINAL BAY 3

Debrief runs forty minutes and produces four lines. Then the others go, Sledge loudly, Patch last, checking the room the way she checks kit.

Rook stays.

He’s not supposed to have the document, and he doesn’t have it; what he has is the photograph he took of it on the fifth floor at 02:49, before anyone told him not to, which is the kind of small disobedience nineteen years teaches a man to commit early and quietly.

He sits in the blue light of Terminal Bay 3 with the rain going sideways past the window and he types the classification line the way you'd say a name out loud in an empty house.

Two answers. Both wrong, and wrong in different directions. A file you may not open is a file that’s there. A file that doesn’t exist can’t be denied to you.

Somebody has been careless, in the way that only people who have never been contradicted are careless.

Rook doesn’t close the terminal. He sits in front of it with his hands flat on the desk, the way the coordinator had his hands on the desk, and outside the window Nova Kaelen is beginning to go the colour of a bruise, and the advertisements are still up, and the lights haven’t gone off once in sixty-five years, because the lights are a promise that the state is awake.

His father's designation was G1-04.

He was fourteen when they told him there had been no operation.
